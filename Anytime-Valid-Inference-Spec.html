Section 1

Problem & Opportunity

Problem Statement

Microsoft runs tens of thousands of online controlled experiments per year across Windows, Office, Bing, Edge, Xbox, Teams, and Copilot via the Experimentation Platform (ExP). For safety-critical guardrails — crash rate, hang rate, latency p95/p99, sign-in success, error rates — teams need to detect regressions while the flight is ramping, not after the experiment closes. Today's ExP analysis pipeline is "fixed-n": metrics are valid only at a pre-declared sample size, recomputed on a daily Scorecard cadence. This forces two failure modes:

  1. The peeking problem. Analysts and PMs do look at intermediate Scorecards and do act on apparent regressions — but the math underneath was not built for repeated looks. Naive sequential peeking inflates the Type-I error rate from a nominal 5% to roughly 70% across many looks, producing false rollbacks, wasted releases, and a steady erosion of trust in the data.
  2. The waiting problem. If teams stay disciplined and wait for the fixed-n readout, real regressions continue harming real users for hours or days while a flight ramps. The blast radius of every bad ship is bounded only by the daily scorecard cadence.

The replication crisis literature (Ioannidis 2005; HBS WP 24-060) names "optional stopping" and peeking as primary culprits in modern false-positive rates. Microsoft pays this cost in two currencies at once: false rollbacks and slow rollbacks.

Opportunity

Anytime-valid inference — built on mixture martingales and confidence sequences — provides Type-I error control at every sample size simultaneously. The math has matured: Lindon, Ham, Tingley & Bojinov (2024) give closed-form sequential F-tests and confidence sequences for linear models with regression adjustment — computable directly from the same regression tables ExP already produces. Lindon & Malek (NeurIPS 2022) extend the framework to multinomial and count data, including a direct anytime-valid Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM) test. Netflix has shipped this in production: ~50% reduction in average sample size and ~60-second time-to-detect on play-delay regressions during canary, gating every client release.

More importantly, Microsoft has already shipped it too. The Windows Experimentation Platform runs the Lindon & Malek multinomial test against WSD near-real-time reliability and Test-in-Production telemetry, surfaced in Mission Control as the Faster Reliability Signals dashboard. That removes most of the methodological risk from this spec and changes its character: this is no longer "can anytime-valid inference work at Microsoft" but "extend a validated Microsoft implementation to IDEAS scorecards, starting with a metric family that matches its data shape." Our first candidate is ASHA (Aggregated Session Happiness) for the Microsoft Copilot App — an event-count guardrail family closely analogous in scope and structure to the TIP measures WExP already covers.

Evidence

  1. HBS Working Paper 24-060 + HBS AI Institute (Bojinov): Documents that under naive repeated testing the false-positive rate climbs from 5% to ~70% across many looks, and frames anytime-valid inference as the operational fix.
  2. Lindon, Ham, Tingley & Bojinov (2024) — "Anytime-Valid Inference in Linear Models with Regression Adjustment": Closed-form sequential tests and confidence sequences that match fixed-n properties at the planned sample size while remaining valid for all n. Empirically ~50% sample-size savings on Netflix A/B experiments.
  3. Lindon & Malek (NeurIPS 2022) — "Anytime-Valid Inference for Multinomial Count Data": Sequential multinomial test, confidence sequences for category probabilities and contrasts, with a worked SRM detection application.
  4. Netflix Tech Blog (Lindon, Atwal, Dimakopoulou et al.): Production rollout case study. Anytime-valid confidence sequences power their canary gating and called experiments roughly twice as fast as fixed-n with preserved error control.
  5. Windows NRT Service Walking Deck (RAM Data & AI Team, Feb 2026): Internal evidence that NRT data is only valuable when wired to a tight "detect → page → rollback" action loop with measurable time-to-detect and time-to-mitigate KPIs. The 4T-events/mo Vortex→AKS→Synapse→Kusto→Fabric pipeline (~$63K/mo for Windows Update Adoption, ≤2 min E2E SLA) is the operational template this product will reuse.
  6. ★ Windows Experimentation Platform has already shipped this in production — wexp_anytime_valid_inference_exp_champs (Patrick Ding, Sarah Shy, Vilasini Subramanian, Ryan Parsell; WSD: Sean Kang, Bhagyashree Kulkarni, July 2026): This is the single strongest piece of evidence in this spec and it is internal. WExP replaced a Pearson chi-square test of homogeneity with the Lindon & Malek (2022) anytime-valid multinomial test, running against WSD near-real-time reliability and Test-in-Production (TIP) telemetry. It is live in Mission Control today as the Faster Reliability Signals dashboard with alerting. The method, the validation approach, and the failure modes below are taken directly from that work rather than inferred.
  7. False-positive control validated on real Windows A/A data: WExP ran A/A tests in Beta-internal and Dev-external populations across 709 TIP measures with recorded failures at α = 0.05, and found the false-positive rate controlled below 5% for every setting of the prior concentration parameter κ they tested. Individual false positives still occur and are documented. This is the empirical result our own A/A validation plan should replicate rather than re-derive.
  8. Downstream processing in the reference implementation costs ~20–30 minutes on top of data arrival: WExP report roughly 50–60 minutes total from device telemetry to a statistic appearing in the Mission Control dashboard or an email alert. Around 30 minutes of that is WSD NRT data arrival, so everything after ingestion — view refresh, inference, alert evaluation, delivery — costs them roughly 20–30 minutes. This is the number that sizes our budget: against ASHA's ~3-hour ingestion floor, a 4-hour SLA leaves about an hour for those same downstream hops, roughly double what a working implementation actually consumes. The headroom is real but it is not unlimited, and it assumes our pipeline is no worse than theirs. See Risk 7.
  9. ★ A complete internal implementation design already exists — wexp_reliability_sequential_testing_implementation (PM Ryan Parsell, Eng Vilasini Subramanian, DS Patrick Ding; WSD: Sean Kang, Bhagyashree Kulkarni; 20 April 2026): A build-level companion to the deck, covering the Kusto object model, the .NET service changes, the rejected alternatives, and the data-quality traps. It states the design goal in the same terms this spec uses: "we recompute at a scale of minutes, so we don't preserve the type I error — the proposed feature allows us to preserve the frequent analysis cadence while also having correct statistical inference." This spec should be read as extending that design to IDEAS rather than originating one.
  10. The recursion has a closed static form that suits Kusto: The posterior-odds update can be unrolled from a per-event recursion into a static expression over the vector of cumulative failure counts per arm. WExP note this "works better with materialized views in a SQL-like system like Kusto" — meaning the inference is a pure function of cumulative counts and does not require replaying an event stream or holding per-event state. Concretely their chain is CfrError17 (raw failures) → WExpFlightCounts (materialized view exploding the flight-ID string and counting by flight × event) → WExpLogOdds (log-odds and candidate p-value per arriving failure) → ExperimentOddsSnapshots (p-value history), read by the monitoring service through a Kusto function.
  11. Ordering of streaming updates is a real, encountered failure mode: WExP explicitly record a class of Kusto-side approaches that did not work: "it's tricky to get update policies to work in order — the updates don't happen in a guaranteed order, so we observe non-monotonic counts of failures." Cumulative counts going backwards corrupts a sequential test whose validity depends on a monotone information filtration. Their landing design instead appends inference results to a history table on a scheduled call. See Risk 9.
  12. Event timestamp semantics are not interchangeable: WExP found experiments whose NRT telemetry carried ingest timestamps preceding the experiment start date, and separately observed client-reported timestamps "from the future." Their guidance from WSD is unambiguous — use ingest time as ground truth, not client time. A related caution: the WSD aggregation helpers return an exact count of events but only an approximate count of distinct devices, since they use Kusto's dcount(). Our method needs event counts, so this is survivable — but any device-denominator metric built alongside it is not exact.
  13. Counts are sufficient — device denominators are not required: Under the inhomogeneous Poisson framing, testing for a difference in failure rates between arms reduces to a multinomial test on which arm the next failure event came from. Only the count of failure events per arm is needed; total devices per arm is not. This materially lowers the data-plumbing bar for onboarding a new metric family and is why ASHA is a viable first candidate.
  14. Structural detection floor in ExP today: Safety-driven flight pauses are gated by the daily Scorecard cadence. A once-daily readout puts a hard floor of roughly half a day on expected time-to-detect by construction, independent of how fast anyone reacts. The actual distribution of regression-onset-to-rollback time has not been measured — see Q7; the range cited in the goals is derived from the cadence, not observed.
Section 2

Goals & Success Metrics

Goals

  1. Deliver a statistically valid regression readout within 4 hours of the underlying event, recomputed on a 1-hour cadence, replacing the current daily Scorecard readout. The 4-hour target is set deliberately against the physics of the data: ASHA telemetry has a ~3-hour speed-of-light from event occurrence to queryable availability, so 4 hours is a conservative budget allowing roughly one hour for inference, alerting and delivery on top of an irreducible ingestion floor. Requires validation and verification: the daily-cadence baseline is inferred structurally rather than measured (see Q7).
  2. Eliminate the peeking penalty — deliver provably time-uniform ≥95% coverage on every published confidence sequence, validated empirically across the top 100 ExP guardrail metric/segment combinations.
  3. Reduce the sample size required to call a safety verdict (rollback vs. ship) by 40–50% on average vs. fixed-n, matching the Netflix-published baseline and returning experimentation slots to teams.
  4. Reach 50 onboarded feature teams within 6 months of GA, with the majority of high-blast-radius Microsoft 1P rings (Canary, OSG-internal, Insider Beta) covered.

Explicit Non-Goals

Replace fixed-n analysis for final experiment readouts — fixed-n remains the system of record for power calculations and the final Scorecard verdict.
Cover non-randomized observational data — V1 assumes IID streams under a randomized assignment.
Real-time experiment design, power calculations, or randomization — those live in existing ExP Onboarding flows.
Build a separate experimentation platform — this ships as a capability layered on ExP, not a replacement.
Cover causal-inference scenarios outside Bernoulli, multinomial, count (Poisson), and continuous (linear/regression-adjusted) families in V1 — quantile and ratio-of-means treatments are V1.5+.
Section 3

User Scenarios

Scenario 1 — "The Release Engineer on a Friday Push" (Primary)

Marcus is on-call for an Edge flight ramping from 1% → 10% on Friday afternoon. With today's fixed-n Scorecard pipeline he would have to wait until Monday morning to see whether crash-rate has regressed. With Anytime-Valid Inference, at minute 9 of the ramp the live confidence sequence on crash-rate fully crosses the regression threshold for treatment and stays crossed through the configured hold-time. A page fires through ICM. Marcus opens the dashboard, sees the shrinking-bands timeseries, confirms with the side-panel transcript ("sequential p < 0.001 since 14:42:31, n = 1.8M sessions, effect = +14% crash rate"), and one-clicks "Pause Flight" via the existing ExP Flight Mgmt integration. Total blast radius: 11 minutes instead of three days.

Scenario 2 — "The PM Watching a Risky Experiment"

Priya owns a Teams call-quality experiment with a new audio codec on the treatment arm. Historically she'd wait seven days for the Scorecard. With Anytime-Valid she opens the live dashboard each morning. On day 1 the confidence band for play-delay-p95 brackets zero — not actionable. On day 2 it's still bracketing zero, but narrower. On day 3 the upper bound has shrunk 30ms below baseline; she has solid evidence the treatment is better and decides to start the next ramp early, returning Teams users to the better build sooner. Crucially: she peeked daily and lost no Type-I error guarantee, because the confidence sequence is time-uniform by construction.

Scenario 3 — "The Data Scientist Tuning Safety Thresholds"

Anya supports Xbox Cloud Gaming guardrails. She uses the dashboard's "back-test" mode to replay historical experiments (including known past regressions) through the anytime-valid pipeline, sees how T2D would have behaved across alternative mixture priors, and tunes the prior to match Xbox's empirical effect-size distribution. She exports a per-metric anytime-valid configuration that the platform applies to all Xbox guardrails going forward, and publishes a one-pager justifying the prior choice to her data-science peers.

Scenario 4 — "SRM Detection During Ramp" (multinomial path)

An experiment ramps to a planned 5% / 95% split but a routing bug sends 4.2% of treated users back to control. The anytime-valid multinomial test on the live assignment counts crosses the SRM threshold at minute 22 with sequential p < 1e-6 at n = 2.1M assignments. ICM page fires, the ramp auto-pauses via the ExP integration, and the dashboard shows the exact crossing event — no waiting for the morning Scorecard, no manual peeking.

Edge Case — "Low-Traffic Experiment, Insufficient Power"

A <0.5% flight in a niche audience produces too few events for the confidence sequence to shrink below the actionable threshold. The dashboard displays a clear "Power building — confidence sequence width still 4× actionable threshold" watermark on the metric, suppresses alerting, and tells the user when the band is projected to tighten given the current event rate. No spurious page, no false confidence either way.

Edge Case — "Pipeline Lag / Stale Inference"

Upstream Kusto ingestion falls behind by 2 hours on top of the normal ~3-hour ASHA floor, pushing total event-to-dashboard latency past the 4-hour SLA. Live Inference detects the staleness from the max ingest timestamp, shows a "Live inference delayed — data current as of [T], 5 hrs behind" banner across affected dashboards, and automatically suppresses new alerts until E2E latency returns under SLA — preventing stale-data pages from waking on-call. Because the readout is scheduled hourly rather than continuously, a missed window is visible as a gap in the series rather than a silently frozen number.

Design Direction

UX Flow

Experiment Created in ExP → Owner enables "Anytime-Valid Monitor" toggle, selects guardrail metrics → Treatment ramps begin → Events stream through NRT pipeline (Vortex → AKS → Synapse → Kusto → Fabric, ≤4 hr E2E) → Cumulative per-arm counts refresh in the materialized view → Inference recomputes on the hourly schedule and appends to the p-value history → Live dashboard updates → On threshold crossing (plus hold-time): ICM page fires → On-call opens dashboard, reviews evidence, clicks "Pause Flight" → Post-incident: replay timeline shows exact crossing moment + transcript

Key Interaction Notes

  • Live Inference tab appears on every experiment detail page in ExP, alongside the existing "Scorecard" and "Setup" tabs. A pulsing dot indicates an active monitored ramp.
  • The signature visualization is the shrinking confidence band: a timeseries where the central line is the effect estimate, the shaded band is the anytime-valid confidence sequence, and a dashed horizontal line marks the actionable threshold. As more data accumulates the band tightens around the estimate; when the band has fully crossed the threshold and stayed crossed for the configured hold-time, the panel flips red and the alert fires.
  • Sequential p-value sparkline renders next to each metric card — monotonically non-increasing under the alternative, color-codes at ≤0.01 and ≤0.001 thresholds.
  • Hover any point on the band shows a tooltip with sample size, effect estimate, lower/upper bound, sequential p-value, and the wall-clock time.
  • "Why was this triggered?" panel on every fired alert shows the exact crossing event, the metric, the effect size, the underlying transcript of micro-batches that drove the crossing, and a one-click link to the historical Scorecard for cross-validation.
  • "Pause Flight" / "Rollback" actions live inline on the alert detail; they call into existing ExP Flight Mgmt APIs and are audit-logged.
  • Back-test mode swaps the live data stream for a historical replay; the dashboard explicitly labels itself "Replay — not a live experiment" with an orange chrome.

Edge Cases & Empty States

  • Insufficient power: render bands with a watermark "Power building — confidence sequence width still 4× actionable threshold. Projected to tighten in ~6 hours at current event rate." Alerts suppressed automatically.
  • Pipeline lag / stale inference: if measured E2E latency exceeds the 4-hour SLA, show a banner "Live inference delayed — data current as of [T], [N] hrs behind" and suppress new alerts until the lag clears, to prevent stale-data pages. Every readout carries an explicit "data as of" timestamp rather than a relative "live" label, so users are never misled about telemetry freshness.
  • Metric not eligible: not every metric maps to a known anytime-valid family. Show "This metric is not yet enabled for anytime-valid monitoring — [Request enablement]" with a link to ExP Onboarding.
  • A/A regime: if the experiment is a sanity A/A, show "Monitoring A/A — expect time-uniform coverage; alerts disabled by default" and surface a coverage-tracking widget useful for self-validation.
  • Tenant in shadow mode: dashboard renders fully, alerts are computed and logged, but the "page on-call" channel is muted. A persistent yellow banner reads "Shadow mode — no pages will fire from this tenant. Graduate when ready."

Accessibility

  • Confidence bands and threshold lines meet WCAG 2.2 AA contrast (4.5:1) against the dashboard background; threshold line is dashed as well as red-coded so colorblind users perceive the geometry without relying on hue.
  • All timeseries are keyboard-navigable: Tab through metric cards, Enter expands the detailed timeseries, arrow keys scrub the time cursor with tooltip readout.
  • Screen readers announce: "Metric Crash Rate, treatment effect 1.4 percent higher than control, 95 percent confidence sequence lower bound 0.2 percent, upper bound 2.6 percent, sequential p-value 0.003, actionable threshold crossed 4 minutes ago, alert fired."
  • Alert payloads include plain-text summaries usable by ICM TTS readouts during on-call handoff.
Section 6

Open Questions & Risks

Open Questions

Q1: Mixture-prior default — uniform vs. matched-to-historical effect distribution?

The mixture prior on the alternative effect distribution directly controls power and T2D. A uniform prior is the safest theoretical default; a prior matched to the historical Microsoft effect-size distribution recovers the Netflix-style ~50% sample-size savings. For the count-data construction we now have concrete internal anchors — but they disagree, and that disagreement is the actual question. WExP parameterize the prior as α₀ = κθ₀ pseudo-counts; their Exp Champs deck operates around κ ≈ 50, while their implementation design document offers "e.g. can use the value 2" and separately illustrates the tradeoff with values of 1 and 100. The intuition is stated plainly in the design doc: at a pseudo-count of 100, ten failures in one arm compares 110 against 100 — barely a signal; at a pseudo-count of 1, the same data compares 11 against 1. Larger κ buys robustness against early sparse noise at the cost of detection speed. Critically, their A/A work showed false-positive control held across the whole range tested, so κ is a power/latency tuning knob rather than a correctness one — which means we can tune it against ASHA's own event volume instead of inheriting a number. Owner: dalyds + ExP Data Science. Current leaning: resolve the 2-vs-50 discrepancy with WExP directly, then calibrate on ASHA A/A traffic rather than adopting either value blind; expose per-metric overrides in V1.1.

Q2: When should anytime-valid be the default vs. opt-in?

Defaulting on safety guardrails for top-tier rings (Canary, OSG-internal, Insider Beta) is a clear early win. Defaulting on production experiments across the long tail of ExP-onboarded products needs more migration support and education. Owner: dalyds. ETA for decision: pre-GA design review.

Q3: Backward compatibility — what is the system of record when anytime-valid evidence disagrees with the final fixed-n Scorecard?

Disagreement will be rare (both methods control Type-I error) but it will happen, particularly near decision boundaries. WExP already observe this in production and have catalogued why: pipeline timing and code differences, a multinomial test versus the Scorecard's Welch t-test, counts versus proportions/means, null-handling and aggregation differences, and nonstationarity. Their own note on it is "two views on reality? Or is one method more correct?" — which is the honest framing, and it means this cannot be resolved by declaring one surface authoritative and moving on. We need a documented policy for which is the system of record in each lifecycle phase — current strawman: anytime-valid governs safety calls during ramp, fixed-n governs final readout. See also Risk 8. Owner: ExP Eng + Data Science, with WExP.

Q4: Compute cost at Microsoft scale?

The NRT deck shows ~$63K/mo Azure for Windows Update Adoption alone. The anytime-valid math is closed-form on existing regression tables (low marginal cost), but the underlying NRT pipeline is the cost driver. We need a per-tenant cost model and an FY budget envelope before broad rollout. Owner: ExP Eng + Finance + RAM/NRT.

Q5: Analyst education — what is the minimum training/documentation we must ship?

Anytime-valid p-values and confidence sequences behave differently from fixed-n — e.g., naively applying a Bonferroni correction on top of a sequential p-value over-corrects and destroys power. Without explicit guidance, sophisticated users will misinterpret outputs in subtle ways. Owner: dalyds + ExP DX. Strawman: ship an in-product explainer (R-23), an internal whitepaper, and host bi-weekly office hours for the first 6 months post-GA.

Q6: SRM detection — supplement or replace today's offline SRM checks?

ExP already runs offline SRM checks on closed experiments. The anytime-valid multinomial test (R-05) catches SRMs live during ramp. Open question: do we deprecate the offline check, run both for a transition period, or treat them as complementary indefinitely? Owner: ExP Trustworthiness + dalyds.

Q7: What is the actual regression-onset-to-rollback baseline today?

The goals cite a ~12–24 hour detection window, derived from the daily Scorecard cadence rather than measured. A once-daily readout implies roughly half a day of expected detection latency by construction, but the real distribution — including how long owners take to act once they see the signal — has not been established. Without it, the headline "~98% reduction" is arithmetic on an assumed baseline. Owner: IDEAS DS. Current leaning: Measure onset-to-rollback on a sample of historical safety rollbacks before review; if human reaction time dominates pipeline latency, then faster detection alone will not deliver the promised improvement and the spec needs an action-loop requirement, not just an inference one.

Q8: How do we define and measure the false-alert rate without ground truth?

WExP defines a false alert as a p-value dropping below threshold at some point and then returning above it and staying there through the end of the analysis period — and openly question whether that definition is too strict, whether it is confounded by genuine time effects, and note plainly that they do not know the ground truth. A recovered signal may be a false positive, or a real transient regression that self-resolved, and the data cannot distinguish them. We inherit this problem exactly. Owner: IDEAS DS with WExP. Current leaning: Adopt the WExP definition for comparability rather than inventing our own, report it alongside the A/A false-positive rate (R-03) which does have ground truth, and be explicit in the dashboard that the two numbers measure different things.

Q9: What multiplicity correction applies across treatment arms, and does it compose with a sequential p-value?

R-09 takes the minimum p-value across pairwise treatment-vs-control comparisons as the headline. Taking a minimum over K comparisons is anti-conservative and needs correction — but the standard corrections were designed for fixed-n p-values, and it is not automatic that they compose correctly with an anytime-valid p-value that is itself a running minimum over time. We would be taking a minimum over arms of a quantity that is already a minimum over time. WExP's design doc notes the need for a correction without settling the method. Owner: ExP Data Science with WExP. Current leaning: a closed testing or e-value merging approach is more likely to be correct here than Bonferroni-on-top, since e-processes combine multiplicatively in ways p-values do not — but this needs a statistician's ruling, not a PM's. Until it is resolved, restrict V1 alerting to two-arm experiments and surface multi-arm results as read-only. See also Risk 5.

Risks

Risk 1 — Mis-tuned prior under-detects real regressions (slow T2D)

Likelihood: Medium · Impact: Medium. If the chosen mixture prior puts too much mass on small effects, genuinely large regressions take longer than expected to detect — partially negating the headline T2D win. Mitigation: (1) Empirically benchmark the chosen prior against a curated set of historical Windows/Office/Bing/Edge safety incidents and publish per-domain power curves; (2) Ship back-test mode (R-17) so owners can validate priors on their own incident history; (3) Ship per-metric override path (R-18) from V1 so tenants are never stuck with a bad default.

Risk 2 — Alert fatigue on Release Engineering

Likelihood: High · Impact: High. Putting anytime-valid alerts on every guardrail across hundreds of concurrent ExP flights risks paging Release Engineering dozens of times per day, eroding trust in the system. Mitigation: (1) Mandatory 90-day shadow mode per tenant (R-15) — alerts logged but no pages until owners explicitly graduate; (2) Minimum-effect-size filters per metric so trivially-small "true" effects don't page; (3) Configurable hold-time so the band must stay crossed for N minutes before paging; (4) Per-team digest (R-21) gives owners visibility into alert volume before they go live.

Risk 3 — NRT pipeline reliability is a single point of failure

Likelihood: Medium · Impact: High. The RAM/NRT pipeline capacity is finite, and an outage in the NRT path silently breaks live inference. Mitigation: (1) Hard SLA + active monitoring on E2E latency (R-06); (2) Stale-data banner + automatic alert suppression on lag (Section 5 edge cases); (3) Graceful fallback to "last known Scorecard" view so the dashboard never goes blank; (4) Joint capacity planning agreement with RAM team and explicit cost attribution per ExP tenant (R-14).

Risk 4 — Methodological resistance from senior analysts and data scientists

Likelihood: Medium · Impact: Medium. Anytime-valid inference is well-established academically but newer in industry. Senior analysts trained on fixed-n may distrust sequential outputs or push to ignore them. Mitigation: (1) Co-author rollout with ExP Data Science leads as named partners on the spec; (2) Publish an internal whitepaper citing Lindon et al. (2024), Lindon & Malek (2022), the Netflix production case study, and HBS WP 24-060; (3) Office hours and back-test transparency for skeptics; (4) Run a high-visibility pilot on a known historical regression and publish the result.

Risk 5 — Privacy & sovereign-tenant compliance review

Likelihood: Medium · Impact: High. Moving event-level telemetry into a lower-latency pipeline may surface new data-residency or compliance questions, particularly for EU sovereign and government tenants. Mitigation: (1) Inherit ExP's existing data classification and residency model unchanged in V1 — introduce no new data classes; (2) Engage Privacy + EU sovereign compliance early in design; (3) Ship per-region / per-tenant pipeline isolation as part of R-14 so sovereign tenants can be carved out cleanly; (4) Make "no live inference" a supported configuration so a tenant can opt out entirely if compliance can't clear.

Risk 6 — Over-fitting to safety/canary; experiment-call use case left underserved

Likelihood: Medium · Impact: Medium. V1 is explicitly scoped to safety/canary detection. PMs and DSes asking "can I call my experiment early?" will want similar tooling soon after — without a clear V1.5+ roadmap the platform risks looking incomplete. Mitigation: (1) Architect the inference engine and dashboard so experiment-call workflows reuse 90%+ of V1; (2) Document a public V1.5 roadmap entry for "Anytime-Valid Experiment Call" alongside V1 launch; (3) Run early experiment-call pilots on willing teams during V1 GA window to de-risk the V1.5 design.

Risk 7 — The latency floor is set by data arrival, not by inference, and ASHA's floor may move

Likelihood: Medium · Impact: High. The V1 target is a ≤4 hour end-to-end readout recomputed hourly (R-06), built on an assumed ~3 hour ASHA speed-of-light. That leaves roughly one hour of headroom for every hop after ingestion — materialized-view refresh, inference, alert evaluation, and delivery. The reference implementation spends ~20–30 minutes on those same downstream hops, so the processing budget is roughly 2× what a working system consumes — adequate, but not generous, and it assumes our pipeline is no worse than theirs. The real exposure is the 3-hour ASHA figure itself, which is an assumption in this spec rather than a measurement. A 3-hour median with a 9-hour tail is a completely different product from a tight 3-hour distribution, and the SLA would breach on arrival. Note also that the inference math is not the bottleneck in either system and never will be. Mitigation: (1) Measure the ASHA arrival-time distribution — P50, P95 and P99, not a headline number — before committing the SLA; (2) State the SLA against P95 rather than median so the tail is governed rather than hidden; (3) Ask WExP for the breakdown of their 50–60 minutes, which tells us directly which downstream hops dominate and where our hour will actually go; (4) Preserve the value case if the budget slips — even a 6-hour readout recomputed hourly is a large improvement over a once-daily Scorecard, so the case does not depend on hitting 4.

Risk 8 — Anytime-valid results will disagree with the Scorecard, and that erodes trust

Likelihood: High · Impact: High. WExP is living this today. Their documented sources of disagreement: data-pipeline timing and code differences; a multinomial test against the Scorecard's Welch t-test; comparing counts versus comparing proportions or means; differing null-handling and aggregation rules; and nonstationarity / time effects. Their own framing of the open question is "two views on reality? Or is one method more correct?" — which is exactly the conversation a Feature Crew will start the first time the two surfaces disagree during a ramp. Mitigation: (1) Publish the disagreement taxonomy before launch so it reads as understood rather than discovered; (2) Prefer test constructions that match Scorecard aggregation where possible — Johari et al. (2015) gives an anytime-valid test for a difference in Bernoulli probabilities that operates on means and would reduce structural disagreement, at the cost of relying on asymptotics and needing adequate sample size; (3) Resolve the system-of-record policy (Q3) before any alert can pause a flight; (4) Instrument disagreement rate as a first-class metric rather than treating each instance as an incident.

Risk 9 — Out-of-order streaming updates silently corrupt the sequential test

Likelihood: Medium · Impact: High. This is not a hypothetical — it is a documented failure WExP already hit. Their design doc records a family of Kusto approaches that had to be abandoned: "it's tricky to get update policies to work in order. The updates don't happen in a guaranteed order, so we observe non-monotonic counts of failures." This matters more for a sequential test than for a batch one. An anytime-valid p-value is the running minimum of an e-process over a filtration that is assumed to grow monotonically; if cumulative counts move backwards, the process is no longer an e-process on the observed sequence and the Type-I guarantee — the entire reason for adopting the method — quietly stops holding. Worse, it fails silently: the dashboard still renders a p-value, and it still looks reasonable. Mitigation: (1) Adopt WExP's landing pattern (R-07) rather than re-attempting the ordering-dependent designs they abandoned, and treat their negative result as a finding we do not need to reproduce; (2) Assert monotonicity of cumulative counts on every evaluation and alert on violation rather than trusting it; (3) Because the p-value is a running minimum, a corrupted low value is sticky — define and test the repair path for retracting a published minimum, since "recompute and move on" is not available; (4) Confirm ASHA's ingestion path has the same ordering characteristics before onboarding, since a different pipeline may fail differently.
